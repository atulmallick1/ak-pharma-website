Walk into two pharmacies in India with the same prescription and you can pay wildly different prices for what is, chemically, the same medicine. A strip of a common drug might cost ₹15 at a Jan Aushadhi Kendra and ₹120 as a well-known brand. Naturally people ask: is the cheap one really the same?

As a supplier that sources medicines for the armed forces and institutions, where every batch is tested and documented, here is the straight answer.

## What "generic" really means

A medicine has two identities: the **active ingredient** (the molecule that does the work, such as paracetamol or metformin) and the **brand name** a company sells it under. Once a molecule's patent expires, any licensed manufacturer can produce it.

In India, the situation has a twist. Most medicines sold here are **branded generics**: off-patent molecules sold under a company's brand name. So the real comparison is usually not "original vs copy" but "one company's version vs another's", with very different marketing budgets built into the price.

## What must be the same

For a generic to be approved, it must contain:

- The **same active ingredient**
- In the **same strength**
- In the **same dosage form** (tablet, syrup, injection)
- Meeting the **same pharmacopoeial quality standards** for purity, content, and dissolution

Manufacturers must hold a valid drug licence, and the revised Schedule M now requires Indian manufacturers to meet GMP standards aligned with global norms.

## What can legitimately differ

- **Inactive ingredients** (fillers, binders, colours), which can occasionally matter for people with specific allergies
- **Appearance**: shape, colour, packaging
- **Price**, driven largely by marketing, distribution, and brand positioning rather than the molecule itself

## So why the price gap?

Jan Aushadhi medicines are procured centrally through tenders directly from manufacturers, then sold through a government-backed network with minimal marketing costs. The Pradhan Mantri Bhartiya Janaushadhi Pariyojana (PMBJP) states that its products are tested at accredited laboratories before distribution. Much of the extra cost of a heavily promoted brand is sales force, promotion, and trade margins, not better chemistry.

## Where genuine caution is justified

Being honest about limitations builds more trust than blanket reassurance:

- **Narrow therapeutic index drugs** (certain anti-epileptics, thyroid hormone, blood thinners like warfarin, immunosuppressants): small differences in absorption can matter. Do not switch brands of these without your doctor's knowledge, and once stable, try to stay on one product.
- **Buy from licensed sources only.** Substandard and fake medicines are a real problem in informal channels, whether branded or generic. The risk lies in the supply chain, not in the word "generic".
- **Check the basics**: the manufacturer's name and licence number, batch number, manufacturing and expiry dates, and intact packaging.

## How to use generics sensibly

1. Ask your doctor to prescribe by generic (salt) name where appropriate; national guidance encourages this.
2. Buy from Jan Aushadhi Kendras or licensed pharmacies.
3. For chronic, stable conditions, stick to one manufacturer once you find one that works well for you.
4. For narrow-therapeutic-index medicines, change only under medical supervision.

## The bottom line

A quality generic from a licensed, GMP-compliant manufacturer, bought from a legitimate source, does the same job as a costlier brand for the vast majority of medicines. What protects you is not the brand name; it is the licence, the testing, and the supply chain behind the strip.
